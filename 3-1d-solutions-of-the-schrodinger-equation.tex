\section{1D Solutions of the Schr\"{o}dinger Equation}

We first note that from the time-dependent Schr\"{o}dinger equation, if the potential \(U\pqty{x}\) is finite at some point, then \(\chi\) must be twice-differentiable with a finite second derivative. But if \(U\pqty{x}\) is infinite, then \(\chi\) must be continuous, but its derivative may be discontinuous.

\subsection{Bound States}

\begin{definition}[Bound State]
    A \term{bound state} is an eigenstate of a quantum system whose wavefunction \(\psi\) is normalisable, i.e., \(\int \abs{\psi\pqty{x}}^2 \dd{x} < \infty\). Otherwise, the state is called a \term{unbounded} or \term{scattering} state.
\end{definition}

Bound states (usually) have energy \(E\) is less than the potential at infinity, and has quantised/discrete energy levels. The term arises from that
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{R \to \infty} \int_{\abs{x} > R} \abs{\psi\pqty{x}}^2 \dd{x} = 0,
\end{equation}
so the particle is ``bound'' to a finite region of space. In particular, the particle cannot escape to infinity. Furthermore, we have \(\ev{\hat{p}} = 0\).

On the other hand, scattering states have continuous energy levels.

Clearly, for \(\abs{x} > a\), we need \(\chi\pqty{x} = 0\), otherwise \(U \chi\) is not finite. We look at solutions to the time-independent Schr\"{o}dinger equation in the region \(\abs{x} \leq a\), which now reads
\begin{equation}
    - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \chi'' = E \chi
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    \chi'' + \frac{2 m E}{\hbar^2} \chi = 0.
\end{equation}

Denoting \(k^2 = \frac{2 m E}{\hbar^2}\), we have the general solution
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = A \sin\pqty{k x} + B \cos\pqty{k x}.
\end{equation}

Imposing the boundary conditions of continuity at \(x = \pm a\), we notice that
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \chi\pqty{a} &= 0 \\
        \chi\pqty{-a} &= 0
    \end{split}
    \quad
    \implies
    \quad
    \begin{split}
        A \sin \pqty{ka} &= 0, \\
        B \cos \pqty{ka} &= 0.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

For a non-trivial solution where \(\chi \neq 0\), we need one of the following two options to hold.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \(A = 0\) and \(\cos \pqty{ka} = 0\), which gives \(k_n = \flatfrac{n \pi}{2 a}\) for odd \(n\) and corresponding eigenfunction \(\chi_n\pqty{X} = B \cos \pqty{k_n x}\).
    \item \(B = 0\) and \(\sin \pqty{ka} = 0\), which gives \(k_n = \flatfrac{n \pi}{2 a}\) for even \(n\) and corresponding eigenfunction \(\chi_n\pqty{X} = A \sin \pqty{k_n x}\).
\end{enumerate}

This allows us to find the allowed energy levels (the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonians) to be
\begin{equation}
    E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 k_n^2}{2 m} = \frac{n^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2}{8 m a^2} \qfor n \in \NN.
\end{equation}

To enforce normalisation conditions on \(\chi_n\), we require
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-a}^{a} \abs{\chi_n \pqty{x}}^2 \dd{x} = 1,
\end{equation}
which solves to constants
\begin{equation}
    A = B = \exp\pqty{i \alpha} \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}} \qfor \alpha \in \RR.
\end{equation}

Of course, we can always fix \(\alpha = 0\) without loss of generality, and we have the final normalised eigenfunctions
\begin{equation}
    \chi_n\pqty{x} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}}
    \begin{cases}
        \sin \pqty{\frac{n \pi x}{2a}}, & n \text{ even}, \\
        \cos \pqty{\frac{n \pi x}{2a}}, & n \text{ odd}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The first three functions are plotted in \autoref{fig:infinite-well-eigenfunctions}.

In particular, the ground state function has no nodes, and the \(n\)-th excited state (which is \(\chi_{n - 1}\)) has \(n\) nodes. The energy levels are quantised, and in particular the lowest energy state \(E_1\) is non-zero. The eigenfunctions also alternate between even and odd functions.

As \(n \to \infty\), we recover the classical limit, since a rapidly-oscillating wavefunction will have a probability density that is approximately uniform over the well.

\begin{lemma}[Parity of Eigenfunctions]
    Suppose that the spectrum of bound states of a quantum system is non-degenerate, i.e., \(E_i \neq E_j\) for \(i \neq j\). Of the potential satisfies \(U\pqty{x} = U\pqty{-x}\), the eigenfunctions \(\chi_n\) are either even or odd functions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    If \(U\) is even, then the time-independent Schr\"{o}dinger equation is invariant under the transformation \(x \mapsto -x\).
    
    Hence, if \(\chi_n\) is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue \(E_n\), then \(\chi_n\pqty{-x}\) is also an eigenfunction with the same eigenvalue. But since the spectrum is non-degenerate, we must have \(\chi_n\pqty{-x} = \lambda \chi_n\pqty{x}\) for some constant \(\lambda\).

    But note \(\chi\pqty{x} = \chi\pqty{-\pqty{-x}} = \lambda \chi\pqty{-x} = \lambda^2 \chi\pqty{x}\), and hence \(\lambda^2 = 1\). Therefore, \(\lambda = \pm 1\), and the eigenfunction is either even or odd.
\end{proof}

\begin{equation}

    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
for some \(U_0 \in \RR\) and \(U_0 > 0\), and \(a > 0\). Since we look for bound states, we require \(0 \leq E < U_0\).

The time-independent Schr\"{o}dinger equation reads
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \label{eq:finite-well-schrodinger-1}
        - \frac{\hbar^2}{2 m} \chi'' &= E \chi, & \qfor \abs{x} \leq a, \\
        \label{eq:finite-well-schrodinger-2}
        - \frac{\hbar^2}{2 m} \chi'' &= \pqty{E - U_0} \chi, & \qfor \abs{x} > a.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Note that \eqref{eq:finite-well-schrodinger-1} gives \(\chi'' + k^2 \chi = 0\), and hence the solutions are
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = A \sin\pqty{k x} + B \cos\pqty{k x}, \quad k^2 = \frac{2 m E}{\hbar^2},
\end{equation}
while \eqref{eq:finite-well-schrodinger-2} gives \(\chi'' - \overline{k}^2 \chi = 0\), and hence the solutions are
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = C e^{\overline{k} x} + D e^{-\overline{k} x}, \quad \overline{k}^2 = \frac{2 m \pqty{U_0 - E}}{\hbar^2}.
\end{equation}

Imposing square-integrability gives \(C = 0\) for \(x > a\), and \(D = 0\) for \(x < -a\).

We look for even eigenfunctions, so we set \(A = 0\) and \(C = D\). Then we have
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = \begin{cases}
        C e^{\overline{k} x}, & \qfor x < -a, \\
        B \cos\pqty{k x}, & \qfor \abs{x} \leq a, \\
        C e^{-\overline{k} x}, & \qfor x > a, \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and imposing continuity conditions of \(\chi\) and \(\chi'\) at \(\pm a\) gives
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        C e^{-\overline{k} a} &= B \cos\pqty{k a}, \\
        - \overline{k} C e^{-\overline{k} a} &= - k B \sin\pqty{k a}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Taking ratios gives \(k \tan \pqty{ka} = \overline{k}\), and hence putting this back in, we have \(k^2 + \overline{k}^2 = \frac{2 m U_0}{\hbar^2}\). Hence, we have a system of transcedental equations
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \xi \tan \xi &= \eta, \\
        \xi^2 + \eta^2 &= r_0^2,
    \end{align}
    where
    \begin{align}
        \xi &= k a = \frac{a}{\hbar} \sqrt{2 m E}, \\
        \eta &= \overline{k} a = \frac{a}{\hbar} \sqrt{2 m \pqty{U_0 - E}}, \\
        r_0^2 &= \frac{2 m U_0 a^2}{\hbar^2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\begin{equation}
    E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 \xi_n^2}{2 m a^2} \qfor n = 1, 2, \ldots, p.

In classical mechanics, Newton's equation reads \(\ddot{x} = - \omega^2 x\), and the solution is
\begin{equation}
    x\pqty{t} = A \cos \pqty{\omega t} + B \sin \pqty{\omega t},
\end{equation}
for some \(A, B \in \RR\).

On the other hand, in quantum mechanics, the time-independent Schr\"{o}dinger equation reads
\begin{equation}
    - \frac{\hbar^2}{2 m} \chi'' + \frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 x^2 \chi = E \chi.
\end{equation}

Using a change of variables given by
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \xi^2 &= \frac{m \omega}{\hbar} x^2, \\
        \epsilon &= \frac{2 E}{\hbar \omega},
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
this reads
\begin{equation}
    - \dv[2]{\chi}{\xi} + \xi^2 \chi = \epsilon \chi.
\end{equation}

We start from a particular solution \(\epsilon = 1\) which gives
\begin{equation}
    \chi_0\pqty{\xi} = e^{-\flatfrac{\xi^2}{2}}.
\end{equation}

This implies that \(E_0 = \flatfrac{\hbar \omega}{2}\), and the solution is
\begin{equation}
    \chi_0 \pqty{x} = e^{-\flatfrac{m \omega x^2}{2 \hbar}}.
\end{equation}

To find other eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}\), we consider the change of variable
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{\xi} = f\pqty{\xi} e^{-\flatfrac{\xi^2}{2}},
\end{equation}
and substituting this recovers a second-order differential equation for \(f = f\pqty{\xi}\):
\begin{equation}
    - f'' + 2 \xi f' + \pqty{1 - \epsilon} f = 0.
\end{equation}

Note that \(\epsilon = 1\) indeed gives constant \(f\) as a solution. Since \(\xi = 0\) is an ordinary point, recall from \textit{IA Differential Equations} that we can look for a power series solution of the form
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:power-series-solution}%
        f\pqty{\xi} = \sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} a_n \xi^n,
    \end{equation}
    for coefficients \(a_n \in \RR\). Differentiating gives us
    \begin{align}
        f'\pqty{\xi} &= \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} n a_n \xi^{n - 1}, \\
        f''\pqty{\xi} &= \sum_{n = 2}^{\infty} n \pqty{n - 1} a_n \xi^{n - 2},
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
and plugging this into the differential equation gives
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} \bqty{\pqty{n + 1} \pqty{n + 2} a_{n + 2} - 2n a_n + \pqty{\epsilon - 1} a_n} \xi^n = 0.
\end{equation}

This gives a recursive relation for the coefficients \(a_n\)
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:recursion-relation-for-harmonic-oscillator}%
    a_{n + 2} = \frac{2 n + 1 - \epsilon}{\pqty{n + 1} \pqty{n + 2}} a_n.
\end{equation}

This gives two linearly independent solutions for each \(\epsilon\), one for even \(n\) and one for odd \(n\), corresponding to our odd and even eigenfunctions. We further require to solve to a \emph{bound} state, so the solution has to be square integrable.

\begin{proposition}[Normalisability of Harmonic Oscillator Eigenfunctions]
    If the series of \eqref{eq:power-series-solution} does not terminate, then the solution is not normalisable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose the power series does not terminate, then by \eqref{eq:recursion-relation-for-harmonic-oscillator} tells us that \(\flatfrac{a_{n + 2}}{a_n} \sim \flatfrac{2}{n}\) as \(n \to \infty\). Then examining the function \(g\pqty{\xi} = e^{\xi^2}\), it has power series expansion
    \begin{equation}
        g\pqty{\xi} = \sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} \frac{\xi^{2 n}}{n!},
    \end{equation}
    and hence \(\flatfrac{b_{n + 2}}{b_n} = \flatfrac{1}{\pqty{n + 1}} \sim \flatfrac{1}{n}\) as \(n \to \infty\). Hence, \(f\pqty{\xi}\) and \(e^{\xi^2}\) have identical asymptotic behaviour, so
    \begin{equation}
        \chi\pqty{\xi} \sim f\pqty{\xi} e^{-\flatfrac{\xi^2}{2}} \sim e^{\flatfrac{\xi^2}{2}} 
    \end{equation}
    and hence \(\xi\) is not normalisable.
\end{proof}

This implies that we will have a polynomial solution for \(f\pqty{\xi}\). So this means that we require \(2 n + 1 - \epsilon = 0\) for some \(n \in \NN\), and hence the energy levels are quantised to be
\begin{equation}
    E_n = \flatfrac{\hbar \omega}{2} \pqty{2 n + 1} \qfor n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
\end{equation}

\(N = 0\) recovers the constant solution, and note that the consecutive energy gaps are \(E_{n + 1} - E_n = \hbar \omega\). The corresponding eigenfunctions \(f_n\pqty{\xi}\) are known as the \term{Hermite polynomials}, and are given by
\begin{equation}
    f_n\pqty{\xi} = \pqty{-1}^{n} e^{\xi^2} \dv[n]{\xi} e^{-\xi^2}.
\end{equation}

The first few values are tabulated in \autoref{tab:harmonic-oscillator-eigenfunctions}.

Then the eigenfunctions are given by
\begin{equation}
    \chi_N \pqty{\xi} = f_n\pqty{\xi} e^{-\flatfrac{\xi^2}{2}},
\end{equation}
and notice the parity of the solution satisfies \(\chi_n\pqty{-\xi} = \pqty{-1}^{n} \chi_n\pqty{\xi}\), i.e., the eigenfunctions alternate between even and odd functions.

\subsection{Free Particle}

We study \term{free particles} with \(U\pqty{x} = 0\). Then the time-independent Schr\"{o}dinger equation reads
\begin{equation}
    - \frac{\hbar^2}{2 m} \chi'' = E \chi,
\end{equation}
and setting \(k^2 = \flatfrac{2 m E}{\hbar^2}\), we have the general solution
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = A e^{i k x} + B e^{- i k x},
\end{equation}
and since energy only depends on \(k^2\), we could label the eigenfunctions with \emph{continuous} parameter \(k \in \RR\) for
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \chi_k\pqty{x} &= e^{i k x}, \\
        E_k &= \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 m}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Putting this into the time-dependent Schr\"{o}dinger equation, we have
\begin{equation}
    \psi_k\pqty{x, t} = e^{i k x} e^{- i E_k t / \hbar} = e^{i k x - i \pqty{\flatfrac{\hbar k^2}{2 m}} t}.
\end{equation}

This solution is known as the \term{de Broglie wave} or the \term{plane wave} solution. But note that this solution is \emph{not} normalisable, as
\begin{equation}
    \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \abs{\psi_k\pqty{x, t}}^2 \dd{x} = \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} 1 \dd{x} = \infty.
\end{equation}

We may:
\begin{itemize}
    \item attempt to build a linear superposition of \(\chi_k\) to form a normalisable solution, or
    \item consider a particle to move on a finite interval, for example, a circle of radius \(R\) with additional periodicity conditions, or
    \item interpret the solution as a \emph{scattering state} as a stream of particles, which we will discuss in the next section.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Gaussian Wavepackets}

For a continuous parameter, superposition is now given by
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{x, t} = \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} A\pqty{k} \psi_k \pqty{x, t} \dd{k},
\end{equation}
where \(A\pqty{k} \in \RR\) is some ``coefficient'' function. A possible option is the \term{Gaussian wavepacket}, defined by
\begin{equation}
    A\pqty{k} = A_{\text{GP}} \pqty{k} = \exp\pqty{- \frac{\pqty{k - k_0}^2}{2 \sigma^2} + \frac{k_0^2}{2 \sigma^2}},
\end{equation}
for parameters \(\sigma > 0\) and \(k_0 \in \RR\). A particular example is shown in \autoref{fig:gaussian-wavepacket}; the width of this Gaussian is given by \(\sigma\), and its peak is at \(k_0\).

\begin{remark}

The constant factor \(\flatfrac{k_0^2}{2 \sigma^2}\) is included in the exponent to set \(A_{\text{GP}}\pqty{0} = 1\); it also cancels the constant term when the exponent is expanded. This differs from David Tong's textbook, where \(\sigma\) is used in place of \(\sigma^2\); here, \(\sigma\) is the usual width parameter (standard deviation) of the Gaussian.
\end{remark}

Then integrating gives us
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \psi_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} A_{\text{GP}}\pqty{k} \psi_k \pqty{x, t} \dd{k} \\
        &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \exp\pqty{F\pqty{k}} \dd{k},
    \end{align}
    where \(F\pqty{k}\) is given by
    \begin{align}
        F\pqty{k} &= - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \pqty{k^2 - 2 k k_0} + i k x - i \frac{\hbar k^2}{2 m} t \\
        &= - \frac{1}{2} \pqty{\frac{1}{\sigma^2} + i \frac{\hbar t}{m}} k^2 + \pqty{\frac{k_0}{\sigma^2} + i x} k.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

For notation purposes, let
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} + i \frac{\hbar t}{m}, \quad \beta = \frac{k_0}{\sigma^2} + i x,
\end{equation}
then by completing the square, we observe that
\begin{equation}
    F\pqty{k} = - \frac{\alpha}{2} \pqty{k - \frac{\beta}{\alpha}}^2 + \frac{\beta^2}{2 \alpha}.
\end{equation}

This gives us
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} = \exp\pqty{\frac{\beta^2}{2 \alpha}} \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \exp\pqty{- \frac{\alpha}{2} \pqty{k - \frac{\beta}{\alpha}}^2} \dd{k}.
\end{equation}

Recall the shifted Gaussian integral
\begin{equation}
    \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \exp\pqty{- \frac{\alpha}{2} \pqty{k - \gamma}^2} \dd{k} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \pi}{\alpha}},
\end{equation}
which holds for any \(\gamma\) when \(\alpha > 0\) is real, and also for complex \(\alpha\) with \(\Re\pqty{\alpha} > 0\). Hence, we have
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \pi}{\alpha\pqty{t}}} \exp\pqty{\frac{\beta\pqty{x}^2}{2 \alpha\pqty{t}}}.
\end{equation}

We substitutes back \(\beta = \flatfrac{k_0}{\sigma^2} + i x\) gives us
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \pi}{\alpha\pqty{t}}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{1}{2 \alpha\pqty{t}} \pqty{x - \frac{i k_0}{\sigma^2}}^2}.
\end{equation}

We introduce a normalisation constant \(C\), and write
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} = C \abs{\psi_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t}}^2.
\end{equation}

Then we have
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \rho_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} &= \frac{2 \pi C}{\abs{\alpha}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{1}{2 \alpha} \pqty{x - \frac{i k_0}{\sigma^2}}^2 - \frac{1}{2 \alpha\conjstar} \pqty{x + \frac{i k_0}{\sigma^2}}^2} \\
        &= \frac{2 \pi C e^{\flatfrac{k_0^2}{\sigma^2}}}{\abs{\alpha}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{1}{\sigma^2 \abs{\alpha}^2} \pqty{x - \frac{\hbar k_0}{m} t}^2},
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
and one may check that the normalisation constant is given by
\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{e^{-\flatfrac{k_0^2}{\sigma^2}}}{\sqrt{4 \sigma^2 \pi^3}}.
\end{equation}

This means that the probability density is a Gaussian in space, centred at \(x = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m} t\), with a time-dependent width given by
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_x\pqty{t} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2 \abs{a}^2}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} \pqty{\sigma^2 + \frac{\hbar^2 t^2}{m^2 \sigma^2}}}.
\end{equation}

We may now compute the expected value and the variance (which is the uncertainty squared, defined similarly to the variance in probability theory) of the position and the momentum. We have, as expected,
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \ev{\hat{x}}_{\text{GP}} &= \frac{\hbar k_0}{m} t, \\
        \Delta x_{\text{GP}}^2 &= \frac{1}{2} \pqty{\sigma^2 + \frac{\hbar^2 t^2}{m^2 \sigma^2}}, \\
        \ev{\hat{p}}_{\text{GP}} &= \hbar k_0, \\
        \Delta p_{\text{GP}}^2 &= \frac{\hbar^2 \sigma^2}{2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

This allows us to see the \term{Heisenberg uncertainty relation} at play: at \(t = 0\), we have \(\Delta x_{\text{GP}} \Delta p_{\text{GP}} = \flatfrac{\hbar}{2}\), which is the minimum uncertainty allowed by quantum mechanics. As time progresses, the wavepacket spreads out, and the uncertainty in position increases, having \(\Delta x_{\text{GP}} \Delta p_{\text{GP}} > \flatfrac{\hbar}{2}\) for \(t > 0\).

\subsection{Beam Interpretation}

Another possible interpretation is that we simply ignore the whole normalisation problem, and take the function \(\chi_k = e^{i k x}\) to be an eigenfunction of \(\hat{H}\). This gives a solution to the time-dependent Schr\"{o}dinger equation given by
\begin{equation}
    \psi_k\pqty{x, t} = A e^{i k x - i \flatfrac{\hbar k^2}{2 m} t},
\end{equation}
for some complex constant \(A \in \CC\).

This is the \term{plane wave} solution. Instead of interpreting this as describing a \emph{single} particle, we claim that each wavefunction describes a \term{beam of particles}, each with momentum and energy
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        p_k &= \hbar k, \\
        E_k &= \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 m},
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
and with the average density of particles given by \(\abs{\psi_k\pqty{x, t}}^2 = \abs{A^2}\).

We compute the \term{probability current} \(J_k\pqty{x, t}\). Recall we used this to prove the conservation of probability back when we first introduced normalisation, using \(\dv*{\rho}{t} + \nabla \cdot J = 0\). This is given by
\begin{equation}
    J_k \pqty{x, t} = - \frac{i \hbar}{2 m} \pqty{\psi_k^* \pdv{\psi_k}{x} - \psi_k \pdv{\psi_k^*}{x}} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} \abs{A}^2 = \frac{p_k}{m} \abs{A}^2,
\end{equation}
and this gives the \term{average flux} of the particles in the beam, which is the number of particles per unit time per unit area.

\subsection{Scattering States}

Now we look at a particle approaching a potential barrier. As a quantum particle, the particle may either be reflected or transmitted through the barrier -- or both. Without loss of generality, we set up a potential barrier at zero. An illustrative example is shown in \autoref{fig:reflection-transmission-coefficients}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig/reflection-transmission-coefficients.png}
    \caption{Scattering off a Potential Barrier}
    \label{fig:reflection-transmission-coefficients}%
\end{figure}

\begin{definition}[Reflection and Transmission Coefficients]
    Let \(\psi\) be a normalisable state. The probability for the incoming particle to be reflected is given by the \term{reflection coefficient} \(R\), with
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{equation}
            R = \lim_{t \to \plusinfty} \int_{\minusinfty}^{0} \abs{\psi\pqty{x, t}}^2 \dd{x},
        \end{equation}
    and the probability for the incoming particle to be transmitted is given by the \term{transmission coefficient} \(T\), with
        \begin{equation}
            T = \lim_{t \to \plusinfty} \int_{0}^{\plusinfty} \abs{\psi\pqty{x, t}}^2 \dd{x}.
        \end{equation}
    \end{subequations}
\end{definition}

Clearly we have the relation \(R + T = 1\). In the beam interpretation, \(R\) is then the fraction of particles of the beam that are reflected, and \(T\) is the fraction of particles of the beam that are transmitted.

\subsubsection{Scattering off a Potential Step}

Consider a step potential given by
\begin{equation}
    U\pqty{x} = \begin{cases}
        0, & \qfor x < 0, \\
        U_0, & \qfor x \geq 0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
for some \(U_0 > 0\), which is sketched in \autoref{fig:potential-step}.

Then the time-independent Schr\"{o}dinger equation reads
\begin{equation}
    - \frac{\hbar^2}{2 m} \chi'' + U\pqty{x} \chi = E \chi.
\end{equation}

In the region \(x \leq 0\), we have \(U\pqty{x} = 0\), so this solves to give
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = A e^{i k x} + B e^{- i k x}, \quad k^2 = \frac{2 m E}{\hbar^2}.
\end{equation}

Note that \(k\) here is the momentum of the particle. The first term corresponds to a wave travelling to the right (the incident wave), and the second term corresponds to a wave travelling to the left (the reflected wave). They must have the same energy, but the densities of the wave \(A\) and \(B\) are allowed to differ, and in general, we expect \(B < A\) telling us that not everything is reflected.

In the region where \(x > 0\), we have \(U\pqty{x} = U_0\), so this solves to give
\begin{equation}
    \chi\pqty{x} = C e^{i \overline{k} x} + D e^{- i \overline{k} x}, \quad \overline{k}^2 = \frac{2 m \pqty{E - U_0}}{\hbar^2},
\end{equation}
where \(\overline{k}\) is real if \(E > U_0\), and imaginary if \(E < U_0\). The first term corresponds to a wave travelling to the right (the transmitted wave). However, the interpretation of the second term is more subtle: if \(\overline{k}\) is real, then it corresponds to a wave travelling to the left, which could be unphysical if our initial condition is that we expect no incoming wave from the right. If \(\overline{k}\) is imaginary, then the second term grows exponentially as \(x \to \plusinfty\), which is definitely unphysical. Hence, we should look for solutions where \(D = 0\) in both cases.

We look at the case \(E \geq U_0\) first, where \(\overline{k}\) is real. Then the whole solution is given by
\begin{equation}
    \chi_{k, \overline{k}} \pqty{x} = \begin{cases}
        A e^{i k x} + B e^{- i k x}, & \qfor x < 0, \\
        C e^{i \overline{k} x}, & \qfor x > 0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and imposing continuity of \(\chi\) and \(\chi'\) at \(x = 0\) gives us
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        A + B &= C, \\
        i k A - i k B &= i \overline{k} C.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Therefore, the coefficients solve to
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        B &= \frac{k - \overline{k}}{k + \overline{k}} A, \\
        C &= \frac{2 k}{k + \overline{k}} A.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Then the probability current is given by
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        J_{k, \overline{k}}\pqty{x, t} &= - \frac{i \hbar}{2 m} \pqty{\chi_{k, \overline{k}}^* \pdv{\chi_{k, \overline{k}}}{x} - \chi_{k, \overline{k}} \pdv{\chi_{k, \overline{k}}^*}{x}} \\
        &= \begin{cases}
            \frac{\hbar k}{m} \abs{A}^2 - \frac{\hbar k}{m} \abs{B}^2, & \qfor x < 0, \\
            \frac{\hbar \overline{k}}{m} \abs{C}^2, & \qfor x > 0.
        \end{cases}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Therefore, the incident and reflected/transmitted probability currents are given by
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        J_{\incident} &= \frac{\hbar k}{m} \abs{A}^2, \\
        J_{\reflected} &= \frac{\hbar k}{m} \abs{B}^2, \\
        J_{\transmitted} &= \frac{\hbar \overline{k}}{m} \abs{C}^2.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

In this case, we define the \term{reflection coefficient} \(R\) and the \term{transmission coefficient} \(T\) to be
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        R &= \frac{J_{\reflected}}{J_{\incident}} = \frac{\abs{B}^2}{\abs{A}^2} = \pqty{\frac{k - \overline{k}}{k + \overline{k}}}^2, \\
        T &= \frac{J_{\transmitted}}{J_{\incident}} = \frac{\overline{k}}{k} \frac{\abs{C}^2}{\abs{A}^2} = \frac{4 k \overline{k}}{\abs{k + \overline{k}}^2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

We have \(R + T = 1\), so the number of particles is conserved. In the limiting behaviour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \(E \to U_0^+\), we have \(\overline{k} \to 0\), and hence \(R \to 1\) and \(T \to 0\). This is expected as the particle has just enough energy to reach the top of the potential barrier, so it is fully reflected.
    \item If \(E \to \infty\), we have \(k \to \infty\) and \(\overline{k} \to \infty\), and hence \(R \to 0\) and \(T \to 1\). This is expected as the particle has so much energy that the potential barrier is negligible, so it is fully transmitted.
\end{itemize}

Now we look at the solution for \(E < U_0\), we denote
\begin{equation}
    \eta = \sqrt{\frac{2m \pqty{U_0 - E}}{\hbar^2}} = i \overline{k},
\end{equation}
then the solution is given by
\begin{equation}
    \xi_{k, \overline{k}} \pqty{x} = \begin{cases}
        A e^{i k x} + B e^{- i k x}, & \qfor x < 0, \\
        C e^{- \eta x}, & \qfor x > 0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where the \(e^{\eta x}\) term vanishes since it would diverge at infinity, as discussed before. Note further that the momentum is imaginary here. Unlike a classical particle, there is some non-negligible probability for the particle to be found in the classically forbidden region \(x > 0\), but it will decay exponentially as \(x \to \plusinfty\). This is precisely the meaning of an imaginary momentum -- that a particle can exist in a region where it would be classically forbidden. This is known as an \term{evanescent wave}.

A wavefunction that drops off exponentially has probability current \(0\), and hence \(J_{\transmitted} = 0\), hence \(R = 1\) and \(T = 0\). Luckily, this matches the solution we get as \(E \to U_0^+\).

\subsection{Scattering off a Potential Barrier}

Let the potential barrier be given by
\begin{equation}
    U\pqty{x} = \begin{cases}
        0, & \qfor x < 0, \\
        U_0, & \qfor 0 \leq x \leq a, \\
        0, & \qfor x > a,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
for some \(U_0 > 0\) and \(a > 0\), which is sketched in \autoref{fig:potential-barrier}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
        \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}
        \begin{axis}[
            axis lines = middle,
            xlabel = \(x\),
            ylabel = \(U\pqty{x}\),

            \addplot[blue, very thick] coordinates { (0, 1) (1, 1) };
            \addplot[blue, very thick, dashed] coordinates { (1, 1) (1, 0) };
            \addplot[blue, very thick] coordinates { (1, 0) (2, 0) };

    \end{tikzpicture}

    \label{fig:potential-barrier}%

we study the case \(0 < E < U_0\), which means that a classical particle will simply bounce back.

Let
\begin{equation}
    k = \sqrt{\frac{2 m E}{\hbar^2}} \qand \eta = \sqrt{\frac{2 m \pqty{U_0 - E}}{\hbar^2}},
\end{equation}
then the form of a solution we seek is
\begin{equation}
    \chi_{k, \eta} \pqty{x} = \begin{cases}
        e^{i k x} + A e^{- i k x}, & \qfor x < 0, \\
        B e^{\eta x} + C e^{- \eta x}, & \qfor 0 < x < a, \\
        D e^{i k x}, & \qfor x > a.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

This is of the correct form, since we have four coefficients, and four boundary conditions to impose: the continuity of \(\chi\) and \(\chi'\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = a\). This gives us the following system of equations. This gives the system of equations
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        1 + A &= B + C && \text{continuity of } \chi \text{ at } x = 0, \\
        i k \pqty{1 - A} &= \eta \pqty{B - C}, && \text{continuity of } \chi' \text{ at } x = 0, \\
        B e^{\eta a} + C e^{- \eta a} &= D e^{i k a}, && \text{continuity of } \chi \text{ at } x = a, \\
        \eta \pqty{B e^{\eta a} - C e^{- \eta a}} &= i k D e^{i k a}. && \text{continuity of } \chi' \text{ at } x = a.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

When we solve this, we will obtain that
\begin{equation}
    D = - \frac{4 i \eta k}{\pqty{k - i \eta}^2 \exp\pqty{\eta a + i k a} - \pqty{k + i \eta}^2 e^{- \eta a + i k a}}.
\end{equation}

Hence, we obtain the transmission probability
\begin{equation}
    T = \frac{4 k^2 \eta^2}{4 k^2 \eta^2 + \pqty{k^2 + \eta^2}^2 \sinh^2\pqty{\eta a}}.
\end{equation}

There is a non-vanishing probability that the particle tunnel through, which is known as the \term{quantum tunnelling effect}. This is a purely quantum mechanical effect, and has no classical analogue.

We look at the case of a lower energy particle with \(E \ll U_0\), we have \(\eta a \gg 1\). The hyperbolic sine function dominates, and we have
\begin{equation}
    T \approx \frac{16 k^2 \eta^2}{\pqty{k^2 + \eta^2}^2} \exp\pqty{- 2 \eta a}.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Summary on Unbounded States}

The de Broglie wave solution is a solution to the time-dependent Schr\"{o}dinger equation and is given by
\begin{equation}
    \psi_k\pqty{x, t} = e^{i k x - i \flatfrac{\hbar k^2}{2 m} t} \notin \hilbertSpace.
\end{equation}

We explored two ways of resolving this.

\begin{itemize}
    \item We may build a linear combination of these solutions to form a normalisable solution, for example, the Gaussian wavepacket:
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \psi_{\text{GP}} \pqty{x, t} &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} A_{\text{GP}}\pqty{k} \psi_k \pqty{x, t} \dd{k}, \\
            A_{\text{GP}}\pqty{k} &= \exp\pqty{- \frac{\pqty{k - k_0}^2}{2 \sigma^2} + \frac{k_0^2}{2 \sigma^2}}, \\
            R &= \lim_{t \to \plusinfty} \int_{\minusinfty}^{0} \abs{\psi\pqty{x, t}}^2 \dd{x}, \\
            T &= \lim_{t \to \plusinfty} \int_{0}^{\plusinfty} \abs{\psi\pqty{x, t}}^2 \dd{x}.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    \item we may interpret this as a beam of particles:
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            J\pqty{x, t} &= - \frac{i \hbar}{2 m} \pqty{\psi^* \pdv{\psi}{x} - \psi \pdv{\psi^*}{x}}, \\
            R &= \frac{J_{\reflected}}{J_{\incident}}, \\
            T &= \frac{J_{\transmitted}}{J_{\incident}}.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
\end{itemize}
