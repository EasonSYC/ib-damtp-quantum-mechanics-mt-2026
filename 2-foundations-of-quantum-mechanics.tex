\section{Foundations of Quantum Mechanics}

We start with an overview of corresponding ideas between linear algebra and quantum mechanics.

\begin{table}[ht!]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c}
        Linear Algebra & Quantum Mechanics \\
        \hline
        Vector (\(n\)-dimensional complex vector space) & State (\(\infty\)-dimensional vector space) \\
        \(\vb{v}, \vb{w}, \ldots\) & \(\psi, \phi, \ldots\) \\
        Discrete Basis \(\{\vb{e}_i\}_{i=1}^n\) & Continuous Basis \(\Bqty{\vb{x}}_{\vb{x} \in \RR^3}\) \\
        Described using \(\vb{v} \mapsto \pmqty{v_1 \\ \vdots \\ v_n}\) & Described using \(\vb{x} \mapsto \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) \\
        Denoted \(\CC^n\) & Denoted \(L^2\pqty{\RR^3}\) \\
        Inner Product \(\pqty{\vb{v}, \vb{w}} = \sum_{i=1}^n {v_i\conjstar} w_i\) & Inner Product \(\pqty{\psi, \phi} = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \psi\conjstar \pqty{\vb{x}, t} \phi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) \\
        Linear Map \(\functiondomain{T}{\CC^n}{\CC^n}\) & Operator \(\functiondomain{\hat{O}}{L^2\pqty{\RR^3}}{L^2\pqty{\RR^3}}\) \\
        Matrix Representation \(\pmqty{T_{ij}}_{i,j=1}^n\) & Operator \(\hat{O}\) \\
        \(\vb{w} = T \vb{v}\) & \(\phi = \hat{O} \psi\) \\
    \end{tabular}

\end{table}

\subsection{State, Wavefunction and Probability}

In quantum mechanics, the wavefunction \(\psi\) determines the dynamics of particles in a probabilistic way.

\begin{definition}[State, Wavefunction]
    \(\psi\) is the \term{state} of a particle, and \(\psi \pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) is the complex coefficient of \(\psi\) in the \emph{continuous} basis \(\vb{x}\) at a given time \(t\), i.e., \(\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) is \(\psi\) in the \(\vb{x}\)-representation, and is called the \term{wavefunction}:
    \begin{equation}
        \functiondomain{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}{\RR^3}{\CC} \quad \forall t \in \RR.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\term{Born's rule} of \term{probabilistic interpretation} gives a physical interpretation of the wavefunction, hence leading to some mathematical property that we will see. The \term{probability density} for a particle described by \(\psi\) to sit at \(\vb{x}\) at a given time \(t\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} \propto \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = \psi\conjstar \pqty{\vb{x}, t} \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}.
\end{equation}
and the probability that the particle lies in some infinitesimal volume \(\dd{V}\) around \(\vb{x}\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} \dd{V}.
\end{equation}

This gives us the dimension of the wavefunction \(\bqty{\psi} = \dimL^{-\flatfrac{3}{2}}\). In addition, this probabilistic interpretation leads to the following mathematical properties.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \term{Square-integrability}/\term{normalisable}: a wavefunction \(\psi\) must be square-integrable, i.e. for some constant \(N \in \RR\),
    \begin{equation}
        \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = N < \infty \quad \forall t \in \RR.
    \end{equation}

    \item The wavefunction must be \term{normalised}, i.e. \(N = 1\), so that the total probability of finding the particle somewhere in space is \(1\): if some wavefunction \(\psi\) is not normalised, we can always normalise it by defining
    \begin{equation}
        \overline{\psi}\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}.
    \end{equation}
    then
    \begin{equation}
        \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\overline{\psi}\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = 1 \quad \forall t \in \RR.
    \end{equation}

    When \(\psi\) is normalised, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2.
    \end{equation}

    It is common for us to denote \(\psi\) for both \(\psi\) and \(\overline{\psi}\), the normalised version.

    \item If the wavefunction \(\tilde{\psi} \pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \exp\pqty{i \alpha} \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) for some constant \(\alpha \in \RR\), then notice that the probabilities are the same, i.e. \(\abs{\tilde{\psi}\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2\). It turns out that this does not affect anything else, and so \(\tilde{\psi}\) is equivalent to \(\psi\).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}
    If \(\alpha = \alpha\pqty{\vb{x}}\) is space-dependent, then \(\tilde{\psi}\) is not equivalent to \(\psi\): although the two give the same probabilities, this changes other observables, and hence are not equivalent.
\end{remark}

In other words, states in quantum mechanics correspond to equivalent classes of wavefunctions, where two wavefunctions are equivalent if they differ by
\[
    \tilde{\psi} \pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \lambda \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}
\]
for any \(\lambda \in \CC\) with \(\lambda \neq 0\), combining the normalisation condition and the phase ambiguity.

\subsection{The Hilbert Space \(L^2\pqty{\RR^3}\)}

\begin{definition}[Hilbert Space \(\hilbertSpace\)]
    The set of all square-integrable functions \(\psi\) on \(\RR^3\) forms a Hilbert space, denoted \(\hilbertSpace = L^2\pqty{\RR^3}\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Square-Integrability of Linear Combination]
    If \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are both square-integrable functions, then their linear combination \(\psi = a_1 \psi_1 + a_2 \psi_2\) is also square-integrable for any constants \(a_1, a_2 \in \CC\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(N_1, N_2 \in \RR\) be such that
    \begin{equation}
        \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi_1\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = N_1 < \infty, \quad
        \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi_2\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = N_2 < \infty.
    \end{equation}

    Hence, we have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 &= \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{a_1 \psi_1 + a_2 \psi_2}^2 \\
            &\leq \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \pqty{\abs{a_1 \psi_1} + \abs{a_2 \psi_2}}^2 \\
            &= \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \pqty{\abs{a_1 \psi_1}^2 + \abs{a_2 \psi_2}^2 + 2 \abs{a_1 \psi_1} \abs{a_2 \psi_2}} \\
            &\leq \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \pqty{\abs{a_1 \psi_1}^2 + \abs{a_2 \psi_2}^2 + \abs{a_1 \psi_1}^2 + \abs{a_2 \psi_2}^2} \\
            &= \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \pqty{2 \abs{a_1 \psi_1}^2 + 2 \abs{a_2 \psi_2}^2} \\
            &= 2 \abs{a_1}^2 N_1 + 2 \abs{a_2}^2 N_2 \\
            &< \infty.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    as desired.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Inner Product on \(\hilbertSpace\)}

\begin{definition}[Inner Product on \(\hilbertSpace\)]
    The \term{inner product} on \(\hilbertSpace\) is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \pqty{\psi, \phi} = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \psi\conjstar \pqty{\vb{x}, t} \phi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}.
    \end{equation}
    with \(\psi, \phi \in \hilbertSpace\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of Inner Product]
    If \(\psi, \phi \in \hilbertSpace\), then the inner product \(\pqty{\psi, \phi}\) exists.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose we have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 &= N_1 < \infty, \\
            \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\phi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 &= N_2 < \infty,
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    then
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \abs{\pqty{\psi, \phi}} &= \abs{\int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \psi\conjstar \phi} \\
            &\leq \sqrt{\int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2} \sqrt{\int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\phi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2} \\
            &= \sqrt{N_1} \sqrt{N_2} < \infty,
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    by the integral Schwarz inequality, as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of The Inner Product]
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \emph{Hermitian}: \(\pqty{\psi, \phi} = \pqty{\phi, \psi}\conjstar\), for all \(\psi, \phi \in \hilbertSpace\).
        \item \emph{Antilinear in First Entry, Linear in Second Entry}:
        \begin{subequations}
            \begin{align}
                \pqty{a_1 \psi_1 + a_2 \psi_2, \phi} &= a_1\conjstar \pqty{\psi_1, \phi} + a_2\conjstar \pqty{\psi_2, \phi}, \\
                \pqty{\psi, b_1 \phi_1 + b_2 \phi_2} &= b_1 \pqty{\psi, \phi_1} + b_2 \pqty{\psi, \phi_2},
            \end{align}
        \end{subequations}
        for all \(a_1, a_2 \in \CC, \psi, \psi_1, \psi_2, \phi, \phi_1, \phi_2 \in \hilbertSpace\).
        \item \emph{Inner product of a function with itself is positive}:
        \begin{equation}
            \pqty{\psi, \psi} = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 > 0,
        \end{equation}
        since the wavefunction \(\psi \equiv 0\) is not a valid state.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Norm of a Waverunction]
    The \term{norm} of a wavefunction \(\psi\) is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{\psi} = \sqrt{\pqty{\psi, \psi}} = \sqrt{\int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Normalised Wavefunction]
    A wavefunction \(\psi\) is \term{normalised} if \(\norm{\psi} = 1\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Orthogonal Wavefunctions]
    Two wavefunctions \(\psi\) and \(\phi\) are \term{orthogonal} if \(\pqty{\psi, \phi} = 0\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Orthonormal Set of Wavefunctions]
    A set of wavefunctions \(\Bqty{\psi_n}\) is \term{orthonormal} if
    \begin{equation}
        \pqty{\psi_m, \psi_n} = \kronecker_{mn} = \begin{cases}
            1, & m = n, \\
            0, & m \neq n.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Complete Set of Wavefunctions]
    A set of wavefunctions \(\Bqty{\psi_n}\) is \term{complete} if any wavefunction \(\psi\) can be expressed as a linear combination of the set, i.e., for all \(\psi \in \hilbertSpace\), there exists a set of constants \(c_n \in \CC\) such that
    \begin{equation}
        \psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_n.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Coefficients of Linear Combination]
    If a set of wavefunctions \(\Bqty{\psi_n}\) forms a complete and orthonormal basis of \(\hilbertSpace\), then the coefficients are given by
    \begin{equation}
        c_n = \pqty{\psi_n, \psi}.
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \pqty{\psi_n, \phi} &= \pqty{\psi_n, \sum_{m = 0}^{\infty} c_m \psi_m} \\
            &= \sum_{m = 0}^{\infty} c_m \pqty{\psi_n, \psi_m} \\
            &= \sum_{m = 0}^{\infty} c_m \kronecker_{nm} \\
            &= c_n.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Time-Dependent Schr\"odinger Equation}

The second postulate in quantum mechanics is the \term{time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation}, which reads
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:time-dependent-schrodinger-equation}%
    i\hbar \pdv{t} \psi(\vb{x}, t) = \hat{H} \psi(\vb{x}, t),
\end{equation}
where the \term{Hamiltonian operator} \(\hat{H}\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hamiltonian-operator}%
    \hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \laplacian + U(\vb{x}),
\end{equation}
where \(U \in \RR\) is the \term{potential energy} of the particle.

Note this equation has a first-derivative in \(t\) while a second derivative in \(\vb{x}\). This is a non-relativistic equation (but it has a relativistic generalisation).

Once the wavefunction \(\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t_0}\) is known at a given time \(t_0\) at all points \(\vb{x}\), then the wavefunction is completely determined at all times \(t > t_0\).

We attempt to solve the Schr\"odinger equation for an \(\Pelectron\). Recall a general solution to the wave equation is given by \(\exp\pqty{i\pqty{kx - \omega t}}\), where \(\omega = ck\). Plugging this into \autoref{eq:time-dependent-schrodinger-equation} with \(U = 0\), we have
\begin{equation}
    - i \hbar \pdv{t} \psi\pqty{x, t} = - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \pdv[2]{x} \psi\pqty{x, t},
\end{equation}
and we get \(\omega = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}\), i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \omega \propto k^2.
\end{equation}

This is consistent with the de Broglie waves in \autoref{eq:de-broglie-waves}: since for a free particle, \(E = \flatfrac{p^2}{2m}\), we have
\begin{equation}
    \omega = \frac{E}{\hbar} = \frac{p^2}{2 m \hbar} = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2 m}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Norm is Constant in Time]
    If \(\psi\) obeys \autoref{eq:time-dependent-schrodinger-equation}, then the norm of \(\psi\) is constant in time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Let the norm be
    \begin{equation}
        N(t) = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2.
    \end{equation}

    We want to show that \(\dot{N} = 0\). Notice
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \dv{N}{t} &= \dv{t} \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 \\
            &= \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \pdv{t} \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    Note that we differentiate to get
    \begin{equation}
        \pdv{t} \pqty{\psi\conjstar \psi} = \psi\conjstar \pdv{\psi}{t} + \psi \pdv{\psi\conjstar}{t}.
    \end{equation}

    By the Schr\"odinger equation, we have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \pdv{\psi}{t} &=\frac{i \hbar}{2m} \laplacian \psi - \frac{i}{\hbar} U \psi, \\
            \pdv{\psi\conjstar}{t} &= -\frac{i \hbar}{2m} \laplacian \psi\conjstar + \frac{i}{\hbar} U \psi\conjstar.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    Then, note that
    \begin{equation}
        \pdv{t} \pqty{\psi\conjstar \psi} = \div \pqty{\frac{i \hbar}{2m} \pqty{\psi\conjstar \grad{\psi} - \psi \grad{\psi\conjstar}}},
    \end{equation}
    and hence
    \begin{equation}
        \dv{N}{t} = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \div \bqty{\frac{i \hbar}{2m} \pqty{\psi\conjstar \grad{\psi} - \psi \grad{\psi\conjstar}}} = 0
    \end{equation}
    by the divergence theorem, since \(\psi\) is square-integrable and hence vanishes at infinity.
\end{proof}

This leads to the \term{continuity equation} for the probability density, given by
\begin{subequations}
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{t} \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} + \div \vb{j} = 0,
\end{equation}
where the \term{probability current} is given by
\begin{equation}
    \vb{j} = \frac{i \hbar}{2m} \pqty{\psi\conjstar \grad{\psi} - \psi \grad{\psi\conjstar}}.
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}

\small

Note the similarity with the continuity equation of charge in electromagnetism
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{\rho_{\text{charge}}}{t} + \div \vb{J}_{\text{e.m.}} = 0,
\end{equation}

\subsection{Measurements and Observables}

The motivating question of this section, is how to extract information from a state \(\psi\)?

\begin{definition}
    An \term{observable} is any property of the particle described by the state \(\psi\) that can be measured. In quantum mechanics, observables are represented by an \term{operator} \(\hat{O}\) on the Hilbert space \(\hilbertSpace\), and a \term{measurement} of the observable \(\hat{O}\) is related to the expectation value of an operator \(\ev{\hat{O}}\).
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Heuristic Derivation of Expectation Values}

Consider measuring the value of a selected die from \(\infty\) rolls. The probabilities satisfy \(p_1 = p_2 = \cdots = p_6 = \flatfrac{1}{6}\), and
\begin{equation}
    \ev{\text{result}} = \sum_{i = 1}^{6} \text{result}_i p_i = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{i = 1}^{6} i = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5.
\end{equation}

The expectation value of an observable \(O\) is the mean of an infinite series of measurements performed on all particles in the same state \(\psi\), given by (in one-dimension)
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \ev{x} &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} x \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 \\
        &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \psi^{\ast} \pqty{\vb{x}, t} x \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t},
    \end{align}
    and
    \begin{align}
        \ev{p} &= m \dv{\ev{x}}{t} \\
        &= m \dv{t} \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \psi^{\ast} x \psi \\
        &= \frac{i \hbar m}{2m} \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x}x \pdv{x} \pqty{\psi \pdv{\psi^{\ast}}{x} - \psi^{\ast} \pdv{\psi}{x}} \\
        &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \psi^{\ast} \pqty{- i \hbar \pdv{x}} \psi,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Heuristically speaking, the expectation value of an observable is given by
\begin{equation}
    \ev{\hat{O}} = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \psi^{\ast} \hat{O} \psi.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Hermitian Operator}

In Quantum Mechanics, an \term{operator} \(\hat{O}\) is simply a linear operator on the Hilbert space \(\hilbertSpace\), with \(\phi = \hat{O} \psi\). Let \(\psi\) be represented in \(\vb{x}\), then our operators may also be represented in \(\vb{x}\) as well.

\begin{definition}
    An \term{operator} \(\hat{O}\) in \(\hilbertSpace\) is any linear map \(\functiondomain{\hat{O}}{\hilbertSpace}{\hilbertSpace}\), such that
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{O} \pqty{a_1 \psi_1 + a_2 \psi_2} = a_1 \hat{O} \psi_1 + a_2 \hat{O} \psi_2
    \end{equation}
    for all \(a_1, a_2 \in \CC, \psi_1, \psi_2 \in \hilbertSpace\).
\end{definition}

\begin{examples}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The finite differential operators
        \[
            \sum_{n = 0}^{N} q_n\pqty{x} \pdv[n]{x}
        \]
        where \(q_n \pqty{x}\) are polynomials are operators.
        \item The \term{location operator} \(\hat{x}\) is an operator, defined by \(\hat{x} \psi = x \psi\).
        \item The \term{momentum operator} \(\hat{p} = - i \hbar \pdv{x}\) is an operator, defined by \(\hat{p} \psi = - i \hbar \pdv{x} \psi\).
        \item The \term{translation operator} \(\hat{T}_a\) is an operator, defined by \(\hat{T}_a \psi \pqty{x} = \psi\pqty{x - a}\) for \(a \in \RR\).
        \item The \term{parity operator} \(\hat{P}\) is an operator, defined by \(\hat{P} \psi \pqty{x} = \psi\pqty{-x}\).
    \end{itemize}
\end{examples}

\begin{definition}[Hermitian Conjugate]
    The \term{Hermitian conjugate} \(\hat{O}\hermitian\) of operator \(\hat{O}\) is the operator such that
    \begin{equation}
        \pqty{\hat{O}\hermitian \psi_1, \psi_2} = \pqty{\psi_1, \hat{O} \psi_2}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

It is easy to verify that
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \pqty{a_1 \hat{A}_1 + a_2 \hat{A}_2}\hermitian &= a_1\conjstar \hat{A}_1\hermitian + a_2\conjstar \hat{A}_2\hermitian, \\
        \pqty{\hat{A}_1 \hat{A}_2}\hermitian &= \hat{A}_2\hermitian \hat{A}_1\hermitian.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\begin{definition}[Hermitian Operator]
    An operator \(\hat{O}\) is \term{Hermitian} if \(\hat{O}\hermitian = \hat{O}\).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Physical quantities are represented by Hermitian operators.
\end{remark}

\begin{examples}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The location operator \(\functionmap{\hat{x}}{\psi}{x \psi}\) is Hermitian. Verify that
        \begin{equation}
            \pqty{\hat{x} \psi_1, \psi_2} = \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \pqty{x \psi_1}\conjstar \psi_2 = \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \pqty{\psi_1}\conjstar x \psi_2 = \pqty{\psi_1, \hat{x} \psi_2}.
        \end{equation}

        \item The momentum operator \(\functionmap{\hat{p}}{\psi}{- i \hbar \pdv{x} \psi}\) is Hermitian. Verify that
        \begin{subequations}
            \begin{align}
                \pqty{\hat{p} \psi_1, \psi_2} &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \pqty{- i \hbar \pdv{x} \psi_1}\conjstar \psi_2 \\
                &= i \hbar \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \pdv{\psi_1\conjstar}{x} \psi_2 \\
                &= i \hbar \bqty{\psi_1\conjstar \psi_2}_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} - i \hbar \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \psi_1\conjstar \pdv{\psi_2}{x} \\
                &= \int_{\minusinfty}^{\plusinfty} \dd{x} \pqty{\psi_1}\conjstar \pqty{- i \hbar \pdv{x} \psi_2} \\
                &= \pqty{\psi_1, \hat{p} \psi_2},
            \end{align}
        \end{subequations}

        \item The \term{kinetic energy operator}
        \begin{equation}
            \functionmap{\hat{T}}{\psi}{\frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} \psi = - \frac{\hbar^2}{2 m} \pdv[2]{x} \psi}
        \end{equation}
        is Hermitian.
        \item The \term{potential energy operator} \(\functionmap{\hat{U}}{\psi}{U\pqty{x} \psi}\) is Hermitian, since \(U\pqty{x}\) is real.
        \item The \term{total energy operator} \(\functionmap{\hat{H}}{\psi}{\hat{T} \psi + \hat{U} \psi}\) is Hermitian, since it is the sum of two Hermitian operators. This is known as the \term{Hamiltonian operator}.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{examples}

\begin{theorem}[Hermitian Operators have Real Eigenvalues]
    The eigenvalues of Hermitian operators are real.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(\hat{A}\) be a Hermitian operator with some eigenvalue \(\lambda\) and non-zero eigenfunction \(\psi\). Then we have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \pqty{\psi, \hat{A} \psi} &= \pqty{\psi, a \psi} \\
            &= a \pqty{\psi, \psi},
        \end{align}
        while since \(\hat{A}\) is Hermitian,
        \begin{align}
            \pqty{\psi, \hat{A} \psi} &= \pqty{\hat{A} \psi, \psi} \\
            &= \pqty{a \psi, \psi} \\
            &= a\conjstar \pqty{\psi, \psi}.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    Hence, \(a = a\conjstar\), i.e. \(a \in \RR\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hermitian Operators have Orthogonal Eigenfunctions]
    If \(\hat{A}\) is Hermitian, and \(\psi_1, \psi_2\) are eigenfunctions of \(\hat{A}\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues \(a_1, a_2 \in \RR\), then \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are orthogonal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We have \(\hat{A} \psi_1 = a_1 \psi_1\) and \(\hat{A} \psi_2 = a_2 \psi_2\) by definition. Hence,
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            a_1 \pqty{\psi_1, \psi_2} &= a_1\conjstar \pqty{\psi_1, \psi_2} = \pqty{a \psi_1, \psi_2} = \pqty{\hat{A} \psi_1, \psi_2} \\
            &= \pqty{\psi, \hat{A} \psi_2} = \pqty{\psi_1, a_2 \psi_2} = a_2 \pqty{\psi_1, \psi_2}.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    Hence, we have \(\pqty{a_1 - a_2} \inner{\psi_1}{\psi_2} = 0\). Given \(a_1 \neq a_2\), we must have \(\inner{\psi_1}{\psi_2} = 0\), so \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are orthogonal.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Eigenfunctions form a Complete Orthogonal Basis]
    The discrete or continuous set of eigenfunctions of any Hermitian operator together form a complete and orthogonal basis of \(\hilbertSpace\) (where we allow for infinite sums).
\end{theorem}

For a discrete basis, any wavefunction \(\psi\) could be written in the form
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \sum_{i = 0}^{\infty} a_i \psi_i \pqty{\vb{x}, t}
\end{equation}
where the eigenfunctions are labelled by \(\psi_i\) for \(i \in \NN_0\), with coefficients \(a_i \in \CC\). If instead, that the basis is continuously labelled by \(\lambda \in \RR\), then we may integrate over a suitable subset \(\Delta \lambda \subset \RR\)
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \int_{\Delta \lambda} a\pqty{\lambda} \psi_\lambda \pqty{\vb{x}, t} \dd{\lambda}
\end{equation}
for continuosu coefficients \(a \pqty{\lambda} \in \CC\).

\subsubsection{Expectation Values and Measurements}

The Copenhagen interpretation postulates that observables correspond to Hermitian operators, \(\hat{O} = \hat{O}\hermitian\), and that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The eigenvalues of \(\hat{O}\) are the possible outcomes of a measurement of the observable \(O\).
    
    \item If \(\hat{O}\) has a discrete set of normalised eigenfunctions \(\Bqty{\psi_i}\) with distinct eigenvalues \(\Bqty{\lambda_i}\), then the measurement of \(O\) obeys a \term{probability distribution} as
    \begin{equation}
        \Prob\pqty{O = \lambda_i} = \abs{a_i}^2 = \abs{\pqty{\psi_i, \psi}}^2,
    \end{equation}
    where \(\psi\) is a normalised state, written as \(\psi = \sum_{i = 0}^{\infty} a_i \psi_i\).

    \item If \(\Bqty{\psi_i}\) is an orthonormal set of eigenfunctions of \(\hat{O}\), and \(\Bqty{\psi_j}_{j \in J}\) is a subset of this with expected value \(\lambda\), then we have
    \begin{equation}
        \Prob\pqty{O = \lambda} = \sum_{j \in J} \abs{a_j}^2 = \sum_{j \in J} \abs{\pqty{\psi_j, \psi}}^2
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

We may confirm that the probabilites indeed sum to \(1\):
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \sum_{i = 0}^{\infty} \abs{a_i}^2 &= \sum_{i = 0}^{\infty} \pqty{a_i \psi_i, a_i \psi_i} && \pqty{\psi_i, \psi_i} = 1 \\
        &= \sum_{i, j = 0}^{\infty} \pqty{a_i \psi_i, a_j \psi_j} && \pqty{\psi_i, \psi_j} = \kronecker_{ij} \\
        &= \pqty{\psi, \psi} = 1.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Finally, we have the following.

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
    \item \term{Projection Postulate.} If \(O\) is measured on \(\psi\) at time \(t\) and the outcome of the measurement is \(\lambda_i\), then the wavefunction of \(\psi\) instantaneously after the measurement becomes \(a_i \psi_i\), or simply, \(\psi_i\), the eigenfunction of \(\hat{O}\) corresponding to the measurement value \(\lambda_i\). This is known as the \term{collapse} of the wavefunction.

    In particular, if \(\hat{O}\) has a degenerate subset of eigenfunctions with identical eigenvalue \(\lambda\) indexed by \(J\), then \(\psi\) collapses to \(\sum_{j \in J} a_j \psi_j\).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[Projection Operator]
    The \term{projection operator} \(\hat{P}_i\) is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \functionmap{\hat{P}_i}{\psi}{\pqty{\psi_i, \psi} \psi_i}
    \end{equation}
    where \(\psi = \sum a_i \psi_i\).
\end{definition}

Then indeed, our definition of the expectation of an observable is consistent with the definition of the expectation of a random variable (in \textit{IA Probability}): we have the expectation of \(O\) measured on the state \(\psi\) is
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \ev{O}_{\psi} &= \sum_{i = 0}^{\infty} \lambda_i \Prob\pqty{O = \lambda_i} \\
        &= \sum_{i = 0}^{\infty} \lambda_i \abs{\pqty{\psi_i, \psi}}^2 \\
        &= \pqty{\sum_{i = 1}^{\infty} \pqty{\psi_i, \psi} \psi_i, \sum_{j = 1}^{\infty} \lambda_j \pqty{\psi_j, \psi} \psi_j} \\
        &= \pqty{\psi, \hat{O} \psi}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Note an important property of the expectation is
\begin{equation}
    \ev{a \hat{A} + b \hat{B}}_{\psi} = a \ev{\hat{A}}_{\psi} + b \ev{\hat{B}}_{\psi}
\end{equation}
for all \(a, b \in \CC\), and \(\hat{A}, \hat{B}\) being Hermitian operators.

The projection postulate tells us that if the observable is measured twice at very small time intervals \(\delta t \to 0\), then the second measurement yields the identical result to the first one with probability tending to \(1\).

\term{Born's Rule} hence allows us to deduce a physical interpretation of the inner product: if \(\phi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) is the desired outcome of a measurement on a particle described by \(\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\), then the \term{probability amplitude} of the measurement on \(\psi\) giving the desired outcome \(\phi\) is
\begin{equation}
    A = \pqty{\phi, \psi} = \int_{\RR^3} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \phi\conjstar \pqty{\vb{x}, t} \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}
\end{equation}
and the probability is given by \(P = A\conjstar A = \abs{A}^2\).

\subsection{Time-Independent Schr\"odinger Equation (TISE)}

Recall the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation given by
\begin{equation}
    i \hbar \pdv{\psi}{t} \pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \hat{H} \psi \pqty{\vb{x}, t}
\end{equation}

Consider using a separable (factorised) ansatz given by
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = T\pqty{t} \chi \pqty{\vb{x}}.
\end{equation}

Putting this into the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation, we get
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{T\pqty{t}} i \hbar \pdv{T}{t} \pqty{t} = \frac{\hat{H} \chi\pqty{\vb{x}}}{\chi\pqty{\vb{x}}},
\end{equation}
but then the left-hand side only depends on \(t\), while the right-hand side only depends on \(\vb{x}\). So both sides have to be equal to a constant \(E \in \RR\), and this yields the equations
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{T} i \hbar \pdv{T}{t} = E
    \end{equation}
    and
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:time-independent-schrodinger-equation}%
        \hat{H} \chi = E \chi.
    \end{equation}
\end{subequations}

\autoref{eq:time-independent-schrodinger-equation} is known as the \term{Time-Independent Schr\"odinger Equation (TISE)}. This is clearly an eigenvalue problem for the Hamiltonian operator \(\hat{H}\), with the eigenvalues \(E\) being the set of all possible outcomes of measuring energy of the state \(\psi\). The time component trivially solves to give
\begin{equation}
    T\pqty{t} = \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E t}{\hbar}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stationary States}

A particular solution to the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation is given by
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \chi\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E t}{\hbar}},
\end{equation}
where \(E\) is the eigenvalue associated with an eigenfunction \(\chi\pqty{\vb{x}}\).

\begin{definition}[Stationary States]
    Such particular solutions to the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation are known as \term{stationary states}.
\end{definition}

The reason behind this is simple. Notice the probability density is given by
\begin{equation}
    \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = \abs{\chi\pqty{\vb{x}}}^2
\end{equation}
is independent of time \(t\), hence the name.

\begin{theorem}[Linear Combination of Stationary States are all Solutions]
    Any solution to the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation can be expressed as a linear combination of stationary states.
\end{theorem}

For systems with a discrete set of eigenvalues denoted \(E_0, E_1, \ldots\) with associated eigenfunctions \(\chi_0, \chi_1, \ldots\), any solution to the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} a_n \chi_n\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E_n t}{\hbar}},
\end{equation}
where \(a_n \in \CC\) are complex coefficients. In particular, at \(t = 0\), we have
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, 0} = \sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} a_n \chi_n\pqty{\vb{x}}.
\end{equation}

If the system has a continuous set of eigenvalues \(E\pqty\alpha\) labelled by \(\alpha \in \RR\) with associated eigenfunctions \(\chi_\alpha\), then we have
\begin{equation}
    \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = \int_{\Delta \alpha} A\pqty{\alpha} \chi_\alpha\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E\pqty{\alpha} t}{\hbar}} \dd{\alpha},
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta\alpha \subseteq \RR\) is a suitable subset of \(\RR\) and \(A\pqty{\alpha} \in \CC\) are complex coefficients.

In the discrete case, in the Copenhagen interpretation, \(\abs{a_n}^2\) is the probability of measuring \(E = E_n\).

\begin{example}[Two-Energy-Level System]
    Now, consider a system with only two energy eigenvalues \(E_1 \neq E_2\). Then the general solution to the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t} = a_1 \chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E_1 t}{\hbar}} + a_2 \chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E_2 t}{\hbar}}.
    \end{equation}
    
    At \(t = 0\), we have \(\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, 0} = a_1 \chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}} + a_2 \chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}}\).
    
    If either of \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) is zero, then \(\psi\) is stationary. If not, then notice the probability density is given by
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} &= \abs{\psi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}}^2 = \psi\conjstar\psi \\
            \begin{split}
                &= \pqty{a_1 \conjstar \chi_1\conjstar\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{\frac{i E_1 t}{\hbar}} + a_2\conjstar \chi_2\conjstar\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{\frac{i E_2 t}{\hbar}}} \\
                &\phantom{=} \hspace{15em} \pqty{a_1 \chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E_1 t}{\hbar}} + a_2 \chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- \frac{i E_2 t}{\hbar}}}
            \end{split} \\
            \begin{split}
                &= \abs{a_1}^2 \abs{\chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}}}^2 + \abs{a_2}^2 \abs{\chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}}}^2 \\
                &\phantom{=} \hspace{8em} + a_1 a_2\conjstar \chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}} \chi_2\conjstar\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{- i \frac{E_1 - E_2}{\hbar} t} + a_1\conjstar a_2 \chi_1\conjstar\pqty{\vb{x}} \chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}} \exp\pqty{i \frac{E_1 - E_2}{\hbar} t}.
            \end{split}
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    
    Since \(\hat{H}\) is a real operator, we can make \(\chi_1\) and \(\chi_2\) real. Let \(a_1 = \abs{a_1} \exp\pqty{i \alpha_1}\) and \(a_2 = \abs{a_2} \exp\pqty{i \alpha_2}\). Then we have
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
        \rho\pqty{\vb{x}, t} &= \abs{a_1}^2 \abs{\chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}}}^2 + \abs{a_2}^2 \abs{\chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}}}^2 \\
        &\phantom{=} \hspace{8em} + 2 \abs{a_1} \abs{a_2} \chi_1\pqty{\vb{x}} \chi_2\pqty{\vb{x}} \cos\pqty{\frac{E_1 - E_2}{\hbar} t - \alpha_1 + \alpha_2}.
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    
    Hence, the probability density points oscillates like
    \begin{equation}
        \cos\pqty{\frac{\pqty{E_1 - E_2}t}{\hbar}}
    \end{equation}
    since \(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2\) is just a constant phase shift. The frequency of the oscillation depends on the energy difference \(\Delta E = E_1 - E_2\).
\end{example}
